Search "vietnamese" in an ad library and you get Dior's Sauvage, a Red Bull can, and four copies of a castor-oil testimonial that happens to mention a seamstress named Linh. Of 211 raw keyword hits pulled from GetHookd, fewer than half were actually selling something Vietnamese. The other half were fuzzy-matched noise — proof that any competitive sweep in this niche has to be read by a human before it's read as data. Once cleaned, 100 genuine ads remained, and they sort into a clear story.

Two categories carry two-thirds of the market: coffee (33 ads) and noodles/phở (35, counting instant noodles and steamed buns). Coffee splits into three distinct plays. There's heritage-authenticity marketing — Bazan, Nguyen Coffee Supply, and Cafe Saigon selling robusta on caffeine content and phin ritual, usually to a US audience willing to pay $50–75 for a starter kit. There's convenience-hacking — JustPour's "30-second Vietnamese coffee at home" concentrate, which reframes the category as a lifestyle shortcut rather than a cultural import. And there's ingredient licensing — Superjelly and Setu putting "Vietnamese Iced Coffee" flavoring into protein powder, and Pioka selling egg-coffee foam powder to boba shops as a B2B menu add-on. None of these three plays compete with each other; they're stacked on the same cultural asset at different price points.

Noodles tell a parallel but distinct story. Chin-Su's Pho Story runs mainstream CPG ads pointing straight at Save Mart and Albertsons shelf placement — this is the instant-noodle aisle, not DTC. Alongside it sits a whole layer of Vietnamese-language advertising that an English-only search would miss entirely: ONICI Ăn Vặt, Weee!, and Saigon Savory run native-script copy timed to Tết, targeting the diaspora directly rather than a curious American shopper. Weee! alone shows up eight times promoting individual SKUs — chè desserts, frozen wontons — the way a grocer runs weekly circulars, not the way a DTC brand runs a hero ad.

The performance data rewards patience over polish. The five "Winning"-tier ads (score 91+) belong to jerky, herbs, and foodservice — small-catalog niches with less auction competition — and every one of them has been running 118 to 270 days. The "Testing" tier, by contrast, skews toward brand-new creative just entering the auction. Read together, the lesson isn't which ad looks best; it's that a genuine winner in this niche stays live for months, and a 2-day-old ad hasn't earned an opinion yet. Shop Now is the CTA on nearly half of everything (47%), and video edges out static images 46-to-42 — this is a bottom-funnel, DTC-shaped niche, not an awareness play.
